As an Applied Math major, I have taken embarrassingly few math-department courses at Berkeley, relative to the CS/EECS courses I've taken (part of the reason for this gap is that I took my lower divisions at CC during HS).

Nonetheless, I have heavily enjoyed the courses that I've taken so far.

## Math H110 (Spring 25)
Comparisons between different fields of mathematics is not necessarily the most useful exercise one can embark on. But if I was forced to make a comparison, I would say linear algebra is the most "useful" subject in upper-division mathematics (whatever "useful" means).

I took 110 with Ken Ribet. I quite enjoyed his lectures, though I often got lost in the sauce (what is a tensor product? God knows). It was exciting to take a class with such an accomplished mathematician. I also appreciated that his version of the course did not involve number-crunching.

We used Axler, a very well-written textbook. One pro is that Axler does his best to avoid determinants. This makes his proofs easier to understand. One con is that Axler does his best to avoid determinants. Unfortunately, I have found that determinants are an eminently useful concept to thoroughly understand.

## Math H104 (Fall 25)
I came into real analysis skeptical of analysis in general. My prior exposure to epsilon-delta proofs made the entire subject seem very uninteresting.

Thankfully, two aspects of 104 remedied that situation: One, my professor, Charles C. Pugh, and the textbook that he wrote, was very focused on topology. Second, I found that epsilon-delta proofs could be more interesting than I gave them credit for.

Incidentally, it was very cool to learn from a professor who wrote the course textbook himself.
